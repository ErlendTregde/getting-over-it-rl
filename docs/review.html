Sixty Hours at the Wall

The agent has had more practice than a human needs to finish the game, and it has never once completed the one move that blocks it. More of the same training will not change that. Here is why, what was wrong, and what to do instead.

Reach is by route projection — the third metric, after two that lied. Nothing has reached cp011 under any of the three.

What the agent is

Soft Actor-Critic with a 44 → 256 → 256 → 2 policy: 20 state values and 24 raycasts in, a mouse delta out, scaled by 13.8. Reward is arc gained along the authored route, monotonic, plus a bonus for touching the next rung. A forward curriculum walks a head rung up a ladder of 38 hand-placed checkpoints; 40% of episodes start elsewhere on the ladder, weighted toward the weakest rungs. The game runs lockstepped at 120 Hz, four ticks per decision, one instance.

Why more training will not work

Three facts, each established by measurement rather than intuition, that together close the door.

1. The lift has never happened

Self-imitation amplifies successes; there are none to amplify. Bursts make a plant-then-swing expressible; expressible is not found. In roughly 4,900 attempts from cp009, zero completed the vertical section.

2. The reward points away from the move

The pogo needs a wind-up — hammer planted below the pot, weight loaded — before the pot rises. Under arc reward the wind-up earns nothing or slightly negative; only the completed sequence pays. Dense reward does not help here. It actively pulls the agent off the preparation, which is why sixty hours found the traverse and stopped dead at its end.

3. The action space is wrong for it, and cannot be changed in place

The agent's mean command is 13.1 against the operator's 0.38 on the same move — 34× — and the pogo is a precision task. But action-space changes were measured to destroy every learned rung. The learned skills depend on the coarse space; the missing skill needs a fine one. That is a trap, not a tuning problem.

Underneath all of it: one game instance at ~30 steps/s means every experiment costs hours, and the physics is not reproducible — identical actions from a bit-identical start diverge from the first step — so telling two policies apart takes 20+ trials.

What went wrong in the work itself

  • The wall was reported broken, twice. Both times it was a metric bug (a radius wider than the rung spacing, no ordering), and both times the operator watching the screen caught it. Three metric rewrites later the honest number was zero.
  • Most of two days went to instruments. Buffer persistence, graceful stops, median-of-N evaluation, champion protection, route-projection reach — all necessary, all now in place, and all time not spent on the agent.
  • Generic levers were pulled when the specific move was known on day one. The operator named it — plant the hammer, push off, swing high — before any of the above was built. Nothing was aimed at it.

What could actually work

A. Shape the reward toward the wind-up

cost: low · honest RL · the one untried thing aimed at mechanism 2

Potential-based shaping on the pogo state — hammer under the pot, weight loaded — so the preparation finally earns something. Policy-invariant in theory; in practice it gives a gradient where there was none. Encodes knowledge of the technique into the reward, which is legitimate and standard, and a narrative call for the video.

B. A pogo primitive

cost: medium · most likely to work · changes what "the AI learned" means

A parameterised macro-action — plant, push, swing — controlled by two or three numbers the policy chooses. Solves mechanisms 1 and 3 at once. A scripted controller that clears the lift can also generate trajectories to seed the success bank, so the policy still has to internalise the move rather than call a button.

C. Parallel game instances

cost: unknown (Steam) · multiplies everything else · unlocks evolution

N instances give N× the samples for every other approach, and make evolutionary methods viable — which optimise consistency directly and have no critic to collapse. Whether Steam permits multiple copies is the first thing to find out.

D. The operator's demonstration, for this one move

cost: five minutes of the operator's time · standard (learning from demonstration)

Demos were ruled out because recording every checkpoint was not feasible. The premise narrowed: one move is the blocker, and the operator has already performed it. A handful of examples of just the lift, captured with the full observation so they can enter the success bank, need nothing else recorded.

Recommendation. Characterise the lift from the existing demo data, then attack it directly rather than generically: a scripted pogo tested in the game first — if it clears the lift, its trajectories seed the bank and the shaping in A is calibrated from real successful states. Check C in parallel; if multiple instances run, the whole strategy changes. Keep the full-ladder, per-rung-quota training as the base it all feeds into.

For the video: the agent climbs 38% of the mountain and stops at the base of the hardest single move on it after sixty hours, with its network genuinely lighting up as it does. That is a story with a wall in it, which is what the best videos in this genre are made of.
